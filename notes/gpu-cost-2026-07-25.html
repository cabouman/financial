Compute keeps getting cheaper — but the curve is bending

A GPU is a terrible commodity — the product changes every 18 months — but dollars per GFLOP is the technology-adjusted price, and it has one of the longest, steepest declines of any price series anywhere: roughly 50,000-fold since 2000. Epoch AI's fit across 470 GPUs puts the doubling time of FLOP/s per dollar at about 2.5 years (FP32).

Cheapest known system (Wikipedia $/GFLOPS table)NVIDIA GPU at launch MSRP

The fine print matters at the right edge. At launch MSRP, the RTX 4090 (2022) and RTX 5090 (2025) both work out to about 1.9¢ per FP32 GFLOP — essentially zero improvement in three years, and street prices ran well above MSRP for both. The steep decline continues only in the datacenter, and only at low precision: an H100 delivers FP16 tensor compute at roughly $25–30 per TFLOP and the B200 at roughly $13–18 (street-price estimates — NVIDIA publishes no list prices for datacenter parts).

Memory is the exception

DRAM followed the same long slide: about $1,045 per GB in 2000 (constant 2020 dollars) to about $1.09 by 2023. Then AI broke the curve. As high-bandwidth memory consumed fab capacity, DDR5 spot prices roughly quadrupled between September 2025 and February 2026 — the sharpest memory inflation on record. HBM itself — the stacked memory on AI accelerators — has held roughly $15–20 per GB for a decade: capacity per stack rose from 8 to 36 GB, but the price per byte barely moved. Memory, not logic, is where the commodity squeeze lives.

The H100 as a commodity

What has a standard AI GPU — an H100 with 80 GB — actually traded at? NVIDIA never published an MSRP, so the record is street prices: about $33,000 at the first public pre-order listing in 2022, $40,000–46,000 on eBay at the April 2023 shortage peak, back to $30–32,000 by mid-2023, and by spring 2026 roughly $25–40,000 new and $15–28,000 used. Cards that sold near $40,000 in late 2023 now resell for $12–22,000.

New / street priceSecondary market

The cleaner market signal is the rental price, quoted continuously across dozens of clouds. It launched near $4.70 per GPU-hour in March 2023, spiked past $8 in the shortage, collapsed below $2 on marketplaces by late 2024, and bottomed at $1.70 in October 2025 — then did something commodities do: it turned. SemiAnalysis's 1-year-contract index was back to $2.35 by March 2026, up about 40% from the low.

Why this matters this week

These charts are the unit economics behind the AI capex debate that moved markets this week: on Thursday the Magnificent Seven lost about $797 billion in market value in one session after Alphabet reported $45 billion of quarterly capex — mostly chips like these — and negative free cash flow. Whether that spending earns a return depends on the price lines above: compute per dollar improving (B200 vs H100), memory per dollar suddenly worsening, and the rental market — the closest thing to a spot price for AI capacity — rising again after two years of collapse.
