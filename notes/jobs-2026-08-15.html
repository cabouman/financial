The July Jobs Report: a Net Loss of Jobs, with Caveats

The July employment report, released August 7 by the BLS, showed nonfarm payrolls falling by 23,000 against expectations of an 83,000 gain. The loss came with caveats in both directions: most of it was a seasonal quirk in government payrolls, but revisions erased 103,000 jobs from May and June.

What happened

The headline loss was mostly one sector. Local government education payrolls fell by 50,000, a swing the BLS attributes to seasonal-adjustment noise around the school year, and economists expect part of it to be revised away. Private payrolls rose by 30,000. Retail trade lost 19,000 jobs and financial activities lost 14,000, while health care continued to add jobs at a slower pace than usual.

The weakness is clearer in the trend than in any single month. Payroll growth has averaged 34,000 per month over the past 12 months, and the May and June figures were revised down by a combined 103,000 — May from +129,000 to +63,000, and June from +57,000 to +20,000. The drop in the unemployment rate to 4.1% overstates the good news for the same reason the headline overstates the bad: both surveys are noisy month to month, and the 12-month picture is one of an economy adding very few jobs.

Wage growth keeps cooling

Nominal wage growth, year-over-yearReal wage growth (deflated by CPI)

These figures are nominal — not adjusted for inflation. Average hourly earnings rose 2 cents in July, bringing the nominal annual rate to 3.2%, the lowest since May 2021 and below the 3.5% forecast. With CPI inflation at 3.4%, real wage growth is slightly negative: about −0.2% over the past year.

The real line is the one that measures purchasing power, and it has moved in a wider arc. Real wages fell roughly 3% per year during the 2022 inflation, recovered at about +1% per year through 2023–2025, and turned negative again this spring when energy prices pushed inflation above wage growth. Slowing nominal wage growth removes a source of inflation pressure; negative real wage growth removes household spending power.

Why it mattered for rates

This report is the main reason a September rate hike now looks unlikely. Before it came out, investors expected the Fed to raise rates in September to fight the spring inflation surge. The report showed the job market weakening, and the Fed must weigh employment as well as inflation. Five days later, the CPI report (covered in the previous note) showed only a small price increase, which pointed the same direction. By the end of this week, futures markets put the chance of a September hike at 42%. The next employment report comes out September 4, two weeks before the Fed's September meeting.
