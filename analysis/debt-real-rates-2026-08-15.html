Federal Debt: Real Rates, Real Growth, and the Inflation Exit

A recent Money & Macro video by economist Joeri Schasfoort argues that debt-to-GDP ratios do not predict government debt crises — real interest rates relative to real growth do. This note checks that argument against the academic literature and against the U.S. data, and then examines how the U.S. actually exited its last debt peak: through inflation.

The claim: ratios don't break governments, rising real rates do

The video's central claim is that there is no debt-to-GDP level at which governments fail. The recent defaults span a wide range of ratios: Greece defaulted in 2012 near 172% of GDP and Sri Lanka in 2022 near 114%, but Russia defaulted in 1998 near 75% and Argentina in 2001 near 65%, while Japan carries roughly 250% today without crisis. What the defaults shared was not a ratio but a spiral: real borrowing costs rising faster than revenue, so that new borrowing went increasingly to pay interest on old borrowing, until the government preferred default to borrowing at all.

The academic literature supports each piece of this claim. Tomz and Wright (2007), examining sovereign defaults since 1820, found the link between hard times and default surprisingly weak — roughly 40% of defaults began while output was at or above trend. Mauro and Zhou (2020), using effective borrowing costs for 55 countries over up to 200 years, found that interest-growth differentials are "no higher prior to sovereign defaults than in normal times"; what moves before default is the marginal borrowing cost, which "often rise[s] abruptly and sharply, but just prior to default." The best-known claimed threshold — the 90% of GDP mark in Reinhart and Rogoff (2010) — did not survive replication (Herndon, Ash, and Pollin 2014). And Blanchard's 2019 AEA presidential address documented that safe interest rates below growth rates are the historical norm in the U.S., under which debt can be rolled over indefinitely without raising taxes.

Where U.S. debt stands

The ratio itself is near its historical record. On the video's argument, that fact alone is not alarming — what matters is the price the government pays to carry it, relative to growth.

r versus g in the U.S. data

The standard sustainability test compares the real interest rate on the debt (r) to real economic growth (g). When r < g, the debt ratio shrinks on its own under a balanced primary budget; when r > g, stabilizing the ratio requires primary surpluses. The chart below computes the effective real rate the Treasury actually paid each year — net interest outlays divided by debt held by the public, minus CPI inflation — against real GDP growth, both as 5-year centered averages.

Three episodes stand out. First, in 1946–1955 the real rate on the debt was deeply negative, which is how the wartime debt shrank (next section). Second, in roughly 1983–1998 the U.S. ran r > g for fifteen years without any spiral — the gap was closed by deficit reduction that ended in the 1998–2001 surpluses. This is worth noting as a limit of the video's framing: for a reserve-currency country, r > g starts a slow squeeze, not an explosion, and fiscal adjustment has resolved it before. Third, 2021–2024 shows r far below g again — inflation, not policy virtue.

Where are we now? On the average rate, sustainability looks easy: the Treasury pays 3.45% on outstanding debt (July 2026) against 3.4% inflation — a real rate near zero, far below any plausible growth path. But the average rate is backward-looking; it reflects debt issued years ago at lower coupons. The marginal real rate — what new borrowing costs — is directly observable in the 10-year TIPS yield: 2.42% as of August 12. Professional forecasts put long-run real growth near 2%, so at the margin the U.S. is now borrowing at r > g. This is precisely the configuration Mauro and Zhou warn about: the average looks fine while the margin deteriorates, and every year of refinancing pulls the average toward the margin.

The carrying cost is already historically high

Net interest, % of federal outlays (fiscal years)

In FY2024, net interest cost $880 billion — 13.1% of all federal outlays, and for the first time more than defense. The Committee for a Responsible Federal Budget projects that under either current or higher rates, net interest will pass Medicare spending by 2029. This is the mechanism by which the marginal rate becomes the average: roughly a third of the marketable debt matures within a year and must be refinanced at today's rates.

How the U.S. actually exited last time: inflation

The debt did not fall from its 1946 peak through repayment. Computing from the same series as the charts above: between 1946 and 1955, the effective real rate on federal debt averaged −2.1% per year, while cumulative CPI inflation ran 37%. The debt ratio fell from 106% of GDP to 53% in nine years, and to 22% by 1974. Reinhart and Sbrancia (2011) call this mechanism "financial repression": interest-rate caps and a captive regulated financial sector held nominal yields below inflation, and they estimate the resulting liquidation of U.S. and U.K. debt at 3 to 4 percent of GDP per year on average over 1945–1980, with real rates negative roughly half the time.

The same mechanism operated, briefly and without the capital controls, in 2021–2024. The effective real rate on the debt averaged −2.6% per year over those four fiscal years while cumulative inflation ran 21%. The result: debt held by the public stayed nearly flat, between 93% and 96% of GDP, through four years of trillion-plus deficits. Bondholders, not taxpayers, absorbed the difference.

This is why the video's conclusion is that a U.S. debt crisis would probably not look like Venezuela or Greece. The U.S. borrows in its own currency and issues the reserve currency, so the pressure-release valve that history shows is a period of inflation persistently above target with nominal rates held below it — painful, gradual erosion rather than default. Britain after 1945 is the template.

Relevance to the portfolio question

A debt crisis is not close. The real interest rate the Treasury pays today is near zero, and a debt spiral requires that rate to be high and rising. What has changed is the forward-looking numbers: new borrowing now costs about 2.4% real, which is more than the roughly 2% the economy is forecast to grow.

This matters for the stock/bond/cash question in this blog's portfolio analyses because of how the last debt peak ended. The government did not repay that debt. It held nominal interest rates low while inflation ran above them, so holders of bonds and cash lost purchasing power every year for a decade. If that is the exit this time, the losses again fall on long-term nominal bonds and on cash. TIPS are the exception: their real yield, 2.4% today, is paid on top of whatever inflation occurs.

There is a second possible exit. In the 1990s the government closed the gap by cutting deficits, and bondholders lost nothing. Nothing in today's data says which exit will happen, or when. The purpose of this note is only to identify what each exit would cost, and who would pay it.
